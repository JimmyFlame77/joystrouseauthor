Think back to a moment in your life from long ago—whether shining with wonder or shadowed with sorrow. Now let your mind conjure its sound. Perhaps music was drifting from a radio, a band playing, or a choir lifting voices skyward.

Now think of the songs your love the most. When they find your ears, do they return you instantly to another time? Music can draw us back to youth, to love’s truest moments, to a perfect summer’s day or heartbreaking goodbye.

Music for many of us is a tether to the past. This is why using a song title or lyric helps the reader anchor themselves into the time period of the story.

## Enhancing the Scene

When writing fiction, we are told to use the five senses to enhance the feel of a place or scene, and what better way to provide a soundtrack to a story than with music? For this to be effective, songs should not be thrown in willy-nilly. Just because your favorite song is [_In-A-Gadda-Da-Vida_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In-A-Gadda-Da-Vida), there are parameters, man. It needs to jibe with the storyline and add depth to the narrative.

(Funny story though—that song is by [Iron Butterfly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Butterfly) in 1968. It was created by the lead singer’s attempt to sing “In the garden of Eden” while intoxicated and is now considered a defining moment in psychedelic and heavy metal music.) So, if your scene has to do with 1960s drug culture, you’re golden. Dig it!

## The Role of Music in Historic Fiction

### Anchoring the Era

Now think of the songs you love the most. When they find your ears, do they return you instantly to another time? Music can draw us back to youth, to love’s truest moments, to a perfect summer’s day or heartbreaking goodbye.

Music for many of us is a tether to the past. This is why using a song title or lyric helps the reader anchor themselves into the time period of the story.

Another way the use of music helps connect the era is its ties to historic events. In [_Beyond the Broken Window_](https://www.amazon.com/Beyond-Broken-Window-suspenseful-friendship-ebook/dp/B09NRWFT5N/ref=sr_1_1?crid=20XJSQ6TFA1BQ&dib=eyJ2IjoiMSJ9.9k7b5W776EKCLj2WAsXW7n2L9US9O1L9k09DtrDKqEU7K5K5B80MOCJtyUa6cH9ViqpUdqt88bEycLibo_eD0y3I3d5ud2rJPi1qQv9kTVMXP3f3myL0IQUAaFDf-8Bu49BYp9Aa-CTubyZTcAEFeJUI9T-dCsrWuoZyMFEjfMHTlmbtyo1e_0aFWlU582ontUBsjTx1FfLLc3LutpMRyG5QIGK40_YY1UkTnX41h_4.Wh4vxdByXqR8Rwdy1bom4lyN0CPGdLkP0Ms_nG5Tzlo&dib_tag=se&keywords=beyond+the+broken+window&qid=1760099869&sprefix=beyond+the+broken+window%2Caps%2C81&sr=8-1), the bartender pours a drink called a Yellow Ribbon, named for the yellow ribbons across the country during the hostage crisis of 1979–1981. The yellow ribbon symbol originated because of the song by Tony Orlando and Dawn.

The first chapters of [_Playing with Fire_](https://joystrouseauthor.proxymarketing.net/playing-with-fire/) are reminiscences of the 1940s. WWII and train travel are both catalysts for events in the protagonist’s life and for most of America at that time. Two of the songs mentioned, _Boogie Woogie Bugle Boy_ and [_Chattanooga Choo Choo_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chattanooga_Choo_Choo), accentuate the vibe of the decade. (Although a fictitious route, this Choo Choo does mention Baltimore in its lyrics.)

Later, in 1954, Eliza and Kate go to the first Jazz Festival in Newport, RI. The performers’ music is described:

> “Dizzy Gillespie’s trumpet blasted out bold syncopated notes that soared above us. Milt Jackson’s vibraphone trembles and wafted. Oscar Peterson’s keys danced in dazzling climbs and complexities. Then Ella sang and the crowd hushed, mesmerized.”

The [1965 Folk Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_Dylan_controversy) is also mentioned—the one when Bob Dylan went electric and was booed for going rogue and plugging in. Eliza writes in her journal, “Guess there’s reinventing going on all over!”

## The Role of Music in Fiction as Foreshadowing

Music is a great way to foreshadow future events. _Chattanooga Choo Choo_ is a foreshadowing of things to come. Our protagonist takes several train rides, one of which is south from Baltimore’s Penn Station.

When she confesses her past, [_White Rabbit_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Rabbit_\(song\)) is on the stereo—foreshadowing her descent down a rabbit hole—then _Break on Through_ by [the Doors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Doors) plays, as if the music itself is urging her to dig deeper.

In _Beyond the Broken Window_, _Don’t You Want Me_ plays as the girls drive to Christopher’s Night Club. No spoiler alert here, just foreshadowing…

## The Role of Music to Set the Mood

### Romance, Yearning, Loss—The Emotional Gamut

Many songs are used to give background to the mood of these characters. In _Beyond the Broken Window_, the gang is dancing to _Rock This Town_ and _Super Freak_—both fun and uplifting. Then the band plays _Jessie’s Girl_ and suddenly Randy is thirsty.

In _Playing with Fire_, romantic (and sometimes steamy!) scenes are accompanied with songs apropos to the lovers’ emotions:

When two lovers share breakfast, the radio is playing.

> “Rock star Eddie Money climbs the chart again from number six, with his first hit,” says Casey Kassem. “Here’s [_Take Me Home Tonight_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Take_Me_Home_Tonight_\(song\)) at number four.”

_Some Enchanted Evening_ plays on the night young lovers meet.

In _Beyond the Broken Window_, Rick’s desires are summed up with the famous line from [_Wichita Lineman_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wichita_Lineman).

A frame of mind can be conjured with music in the air:

-   Young Bonnie hearing _Far Away Places_ wafting out the door of the Marble Hill Inn. The reader may not know the song, but the feeling is there just from its title and her reactions.
-   Julie in _Beyond the Broken Window_ hears Craig singing [_Try a Little Tenderness_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Try_a_Little_Tenderness) and the song cracks open a well of misery, heartache, and shame that she has been pushing away for so long.
-   Joe sings and dances along with _Walking on Sunshine_, catches his reflection in the window, and cracks up laughing. “And it makes me feel good!”

## Music’s Impact on Fiction

In summary, the role of music in fiction is to anchor, solidify, enlighten, and enhance. If used wisely, the written words will spring from the page like a brass band or trickle into the reader’s consciousness soft as a lullaby.

And sometimes it just makes you feel good!

## Music in the Pages of _Playing With Fire_

The story world is threaded with music from every era, beginning with classics like _Just Because_, _Chattanooga Choo Choo_ by Glenn Miller and His Orchestra, _Girl On Fire_ by Alicia Keys, _Boogie Woogie Bugle Boy_ by The Andrews Sisters, _Out of Limits_ by The Markettes, _Swinging on a Star_ by Bing Crosby, _A Shanty in Old Shantytown_ by The Ink Spots, _Tutti Frutti_ by Little Richard, _Far Away Places_ by Margaret Whiting, _Sixty Minute Man_ by Billy Ward, _Honey Love_ by Clyde McPhatter and The Drifters, _At My Front Door_ by The El Dorados, _Shake, Rattle and Roll_ by Bill Haley & His Comets, _C’est Si Bon_ by Eartha Kitt, _Mess Around_ by Ray Charles, and _Summertime_ by Sam Cooke. The 1960s and 1970s bring in _Tell Her No_ by The Zombies, _White Rabbit_ by Jefferson Airplane, _Like a Rolling Stone_ and _It Ain’t Me, Babe_ by Bob Dylan, _Time Is On My Side_ by The Rolling Stones, _Break On Through_ by The Doors, _Into the Mystic_ by Van Morrison, _Somewhere My Love_ by Frank Sinatra (Prime – Jack Jones), _Bring It On Home_ by Sam Cooke, _Love Reign O’er Me_ by The Doors, _Morning Has Broken_ by Cat Stevens, _This Old Heart of Mine_ by both The Isley Brothers and The Supremes, _Goodnight, Irene_ by Willie Nelson and Tom Petty, and the 1980s add _Walking on Sunshine_ by Katrina and the Waves, _Invisible Touch_ by Genesis, _Take Me Home Tonight_ by Eddie Money, _Saving All My Love for You_ by Whitney Houston, and _I Still Haven’t Found What I’m Looking For_ by U2. The Jazz Festival selections include _Someone to Watch Over Me_ by Ella Fitzgerald, _Con Alma_ by Dizzy Gillespie, _What’s New_ by Milt Jackson, and _Georgia on My Mind_ by The Oscar Peterson Trio. Broadway moments feature _Blow, Gabriel, Blow_ from _Anything Goes_, _Rain in Spain_, _Just You Wait_, and _Get Me to the Church on Time_ from _My Fair Lady_, along with _Some Enchanted Evening_ from _South Pacific_ (1995 version – The Temptations).
